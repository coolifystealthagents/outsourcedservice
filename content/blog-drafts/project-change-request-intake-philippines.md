Project changes often begin in harmless language: “Could we also add this field?” or “Can the launch move up a week?” When the request stays in chat, teams can start work without agreeing on cost, timing, acceptance criteria, or authority. Good change administration makes the decision visible before activity outruns approval.

A Philippines-based operations specialist can convert informal requests into complete, traceable packets. They record the request, link it to the approved baseline, gather impact inputs, coordinate review, and maintain the decision history. Scope, price, schedule, contractual interpretation, risk acceptance, and customer commitments remain with project owners.

## Capture the request without accepting it

Give every request an identifier and record who asked, when, through which channel, and in what words. Acknowledge receipt without implying approval: “We have logged the request for impact review” is different from “We will add it.”

Link the affected statement of work, requirement, milestone, or acceptance criterion. If no baseline exists, flag that gap. The specialist should not decide what the parties “must have meant.” Preserve relevant messages and ask the owner to identify the controlling record.

Record the desired outcome separately from the proposed solution. A customer asking for a new dashboard may actually need a weekly exception view. The project team should evaluate options, but the intake coordinator should not redesign the deliverable while transcribing the request.

## Ask structured impact questions

Route the request to the owners of delivery, commercial terms, security, data, operations, and customer success as applicable. Ask each for impact within their authority: affected work, dependency, estimate basis, resource constraint, risk, testing need, and decision deadline.

Do not combine estimates casually. Five days of engineering and two days of testing may overlap or may be sequential. The packet should preserve each contributor's assumptions and confidence. Label unknowns rather than converting them to zero.

GAO's internal-control framework emphasizes quality information and documented controls. A change packet should let a decision-maker trace claims to their owner. It is not enough to report “low impact” without showing who assessed which dimension.

## Develop options without manufacturing approval

Owners may propose accept now, defer, exchange for other scope, run discovery first, or decline. Present each option with its consequences and assumptions. The coordinator can standardize the comparison but should not choose the preferred option.

Make dependencies explicit. An earlier launch may require reducing scope; a new integration may require a security review; a wording change may affect training and support. Include “do nothing” or “retain baseline” so the decision does not appear forced.

If commercial terms may change, keep draft figures and negotiation notes restricted. Only an authorized commercial owner should communicate price or contractual effect. The coordinator schedules the review and records the approved communication.

## Maintain a decision register

The decision record should state the authorized decision-maker, decision time, approved option, affected baseline, conditions, funding or commercial reference, and effective date. A thumbs-up emoji may prompt follow-up, but it should not become approval unless policy explicitly recognizes it and the identity and scope are unambiguous.

Rejected and deferred requests remain valuable records. Capture the reason at the level the owner approved and any revisit trigger. Do not delete the request simply because it did not enter delivery.

Records-management guidance from the U.S. National Archives underscores preserving context and managing records consistently. Follow the organization's retention policy rather than scattering screenshots across personal folders.

## Control work after the decision

Once approved, update the baseline through the designated owner and system. Link new requirements, schedule, budget, test plan, and communications back to the change identifier. The specialist can check that required artifacts changed, but should not alter commercial or delivery commitments without approval.

If work appears to have started before approval, record the observation and escalate. Do not retroactively label the request approved to make the dashboard clean. The owner decides whether to stop work, authorize it, or address the exception.

NIST SP 800-53 includes configuration and change-control concepts relevant to controlled systems. Project change control is broader, yet the same discipline helps: define authorization, assess effects, record implementation, and review results.

## Make customer communication exact

Every update should reflect the current state: logged, under assessment, awaiting decision, approved with conditions, declined, or implemented. Avoid “the team is working on it” when only assessment is underway. State the next expected update without promising the decision.

If the customer adds new details, preserve them as a revision or linked request. Do not overwrite the original because the difference may explain a changed estimate. Confirm which version the owner decided.

Before closure, verify that the decision came from an authorized role, affected baselines were updated, teams received the same approved version, acceptance criteria exist, and implementation evidence is linked. The coordinator may gather sign-off evidence; they do not decide that the customer must accept incomplete work.

Useful measures include requests with complete intake, time waiting for owner input, work discovered before approval, packets returned for missing impact, implementation aligned to approved version, and stale deferred decisions. Avoid praising a low number of change requests; that can drive legitimate changes underground.

Start with one project and collect recent changes from chat, email, and meetings. Reconstruct what the process should have recorded, then create a single intake route. A bounded [operations support role](/services/operations-support) can make change decisions faster and clearer without gaining authority over scope, money, risk, contracts, or commitments.
